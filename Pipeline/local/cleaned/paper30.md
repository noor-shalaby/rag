## Research Article
## Diagnostic value of serum hyperbilirubinemia as an early important predictive factor for complicated appendicitis: Minia University Hospital experience
<!-- image -->
Mohamed Khalaf, Ahmed Attia 1 , Asaad Abdelrhman 1 , Ahmed Mohamed Kelany 1 , and Michael Remon Roshdy Tawadros 1
1 Department of Surgery, Faculty of Medicine, Minia University, Minia, Egypt
DOI: 10.21608/MJMR.2025.434109.2114
Submit Date: 2025-10-19
## Abstract
Background: Acute appendicitis is common and time-critical, yet reliable differentiation between uncomplicated and complicated disease at first contact remains challenging, particularly where imaging access and senior review vary. Objective: To determine whether serum direct hyperbilirubinemia functions as an early indicator of complicated appendicitis and to clarify its role within routine preoperative assessment. Methods: We conducted a prospective study in the emergency surgery unit at Minia University Hospital, enrolling 400 consecutive patients with clinical features of acute appendicitis. Patients were classified as uncomplicated or complicated (perforated/gangrenous) using operative and histopathological criteria. Preoperative assessment included clinical evaluation and routine laboratories, with direct bilirubin measured alongside standard indices. Diagnostic performance was estimated using receiver operating characteristic analysis with area under the curve (AUC) and threshold-based accuracy. Results: Higher direct bilirubin values were associated with complicated appendicitis. Direct bilirubin showed good accuracy (AUC: 0.71, CI: 0.64-0.79, p&lt;0.001*) with a cutoff &gt;0.39 mg/dL yielding 72% sensitivity and 51% specificity. Interpreted with vital signs and routine laboratory markers, bilirubin added selective, adjunctive information for early risk stratification. Conclusion: Serum direct bilirubin provides a practical adjunct to early assessment of suspected appendicitis, supporting faster and more proportionate decisions-earlier escalation when overall risk is high and safe observation when it is low. Because testing is rapid, inexpensive, and universally available, integration into local diagnostic pathways is feasible, including in resource-constrained settings.
Keywords: acute appendicitis; bilirubin; complicated appendicitis prediction; Hyperbilirubinemia biomarker.
## Introduction
Acute appendicitis is a leading global surgical emergency, most often triggered by luminal obstruction (e.g., faecolith, lymphoid hyperplasia). Obstruction converts the appendix into a closed loop: ongoing mucosal secretion and gas accumulation cause rapid distension, leading first to venous congestion, then mucosal ischaemia and bacterial overgrowth/
translocation. The process progresses to suppurative inflammation, and-if unchecked -transmural necrosis (gangrene) with either contained abscess/phlegmon or free perforation and peritonitis (1 ). Risk of perforation rises with increasing symptom duration. Accordingly, earlier diagnosis and timely appendicectomy are associated with a lower probability of perforation and its septic complications (2) .

Appendicitis peaks in adolescence and young adulthood-approximately the second to fourth decades-yet it can occur at any age (3). Fewer than 5% of cases are diagnosed in children under five years, whereas about 40% present in individuals during second and third decades of life (4) . In the United States, the estimated lifetime risk of appendicitis is ~8.6% in males and ~6.7% in females. At operation, complicated appendicitis-for example gangrene, perforation, or periappendiceal abscess/ phlegmon-is reported in roughly 16.5-24.4% of cases (5).
Roughly 75-80% of hepatic blood flow comes via the portal vein, which delivers gut-derived bacteria, endotoxin, and absorbed solutes. In complicated appendicitis, the bacterial/ endotoxin load entering the portal circulation can outstrip hepatic clearance, producing sepsis-associated cholestasis and hepatocellular injury with a rise in serum bilirubin. In parallel, Kupffer cells/ macrophages release proinflammatory cytokines-notably TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6-that impair canalicular excretion of conjugated bilirubin, further driving hyperbilirubinaemia (6-8).
Sreeramulu &amp; Shashidhar noted that direct (conjugated) hyperbilirubinaemia may help predict gangrene or perforation (9) .
Within Upper Egypt-and specifically Minia Governorate-we found no prior published studies addressing this question; accordingly, this study is designed as the first evaluation of the association between total/direct bilirubin and acute complicated appendicitis in this population.
## Patients and Methods
This prospective study was conducted in the emergency surgery unit at Minia University Hospital, involving 400 consecutive patients (both adults and children) presenting with clinical features of acute appendicitis between January 2025 and September 2025.
Ethical consideration : Institutional Review Board approval was obtained before study initiation (Approval No. 1360/01/2025). All procedures adhered to Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Participants were informed about the study and provided written informed consent. Data were kept confidential, and no personally identifying information appears in any reports or publications.
Inclusion criteria: All patients presented with right iliac fossa pain diagnosed clinically as AA and had undergone appendectomy (open or lap).
Exclusion Criteria: patients with normal appendix which was proved by postoperative histopathological examination and those with past history of jaundice and other causes of hyperbilirubinemia (hemolytic disease, positive hepatitis viruses, cholelithiasis, acquired or congenital biliary disease, and cancer of the hepatobiliary system)
## Study Design
Upon admission, Patients were subjected to full history taking including personal history, Medical history and Surgical history and Clinical examination including General examination and Local examination, assessment of hypertension, DM. Routine laboratory tests before administration of iv antibiotics, including complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests (specifically total and direct bilirubin levels), Prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin concentration, the International Normalized Ratio (INR) and Random blood sugar, were performed as part. The primary outcome measure was the association between serum bilirubin levels (direct) and the severity of appendicitis (complicated vs. non-complicated).
## The surgical procedure:
All procedures were performed under general or spinal anaesthesia. All patients received a prophylactic dose of antibiotics half an hour before the start of procedure. The patients were performed via the open or laparoscopic approaches. Any purulent fluid was aspirated, and good peritoneal toilet was done. The wound was closed in layers in the open cases, while the skin at the site of the ports was closed in the laparoscopic cases.
Patients were classified into two groups:
- Simple appendicitis group (A) : Cases where no perforation, gangrene, or other complications were found during surgery.
- Complicated appendicitis group (B) : Patients who presented with perforation or
gangrene, confirmed via operative findings and histopathological examination.
## Diagnostic Evaluation
direct bilirubin levels were measured preoperatively as part of the routine laboratory workup. Other laboratory tests, including WBC count, and vital signs (temperature, pulse rate, blood pressure), were recorded. Imaging, including ultrasound and CT scans, were performed when clinically indicated, particularly for atypical presentations.
## Surgical and Histopathological Classification
All patients underwent surgery, and the type of appendicitis was classified based on operative and histopathological findings. The appendices were examined for signs of perforation and gangrene. The final diagnosis was confirmed through histopathological evaluation of the excised appendix.

## Discussion
Acute appendicitis represents the most frequent cause of acute abdominal pain, yet there remains no single laboratory investigation that can confirm the diagnosis with certainty ( 10 ) . Diagnostic uncertainty occasionally arises because appendicitis does not always present with typical clinical features, and right iliac fossa pain has a wide range of differential causes, more so in women ( 11 ) . Failure to recognize and treat appendicitis promptly may culminate in perforation with peritoneal involvement, which is associated with significant morbidity and longer inpatient recovery ( 12 ) . The incidence of appendiceal perforation is on the rise, currently documented between 13-37%. These complications substantially heighten morbidity, with wound infection or peritonitis affecting nearly half of patients (47%), and mortality recorded at approximately 3% ( 13 ) . Therefore, there is growing interest in identifying reliable laboratory markers to support the diagnosis.
The present study was designed to investigate whether serum hyperbilirubinemia can serve as a reliable early predictor of complicated appendicitis. Our results demonstrated a clear and consistent association between elevated bilirubin levels and the presence of perforated or gangrenous appendicitis, thereby reaffirming the biomarker's diagnostic relevance.
The mean age in our study population (400 patients) was 29.6±11.7 years. This is consistent with the quoted incidence of Appendicitis in the literature where it is most frequently seen in patients in their second through fourth decades of life ( 14 ) . In our study, age was a significant differentiator between the two groups. Patients with complicated appendicitis were older (mean age 32.4 ± 13.8 years) compared to those with simple appendicitis (mean age 28.3 ± 10.3 years, p=0.001). This aligns with Yuan et al., (2022) and Lapsa et al., (2021), who found that older patients are more likely to experience complicated appendicitis, often due to delayed diagnosis or atypical symptom presentation. Additionally, older patients may have reduced immune response and other comorbidities, further complicating their ability to fight infection and preventing early resolution of appendicitis ( 15 , 16 ) .
The gender distribution in this study was nearly equal, with 199 males (49.8%) and 201 females (50.2%). There was no significant difference between the groups (p=0.41), showing a slight male predominance in both simple and complicated appendicitis cases. This suggests that sex does not significantly impact the risk of complications in appendicitis, which contrasts with findings by Kollias et al., (2024), who reported a higher incidence of appendicitis and complications like perforation or gangrene in males ( 17 ) .
The mean temperature for the study population (400 patients) was 37.7 ± 0.29°C. Patients with complicated appendicitis had a significantly higher temperature (38.1 ± 0.17°C) than those with simple appendicitis (37.6 ± 0.10°C, p&lt;0.001). This suggests that fever may indicate severe infection or systemic inflammation, which are common in complicated appendicitis. Similarly, the mean pulse rate was 75.5 ± 10.7 bpm. The pulse was significantly higher in the complicated appendicitis group (90.2 ± 3.6 bpm) compared to the simple appendicitis group (68.6 ± 3.6 bpm, p&lt;0.001). Elevated pulse rates are typically seen in systemic infections, reflecting the body's response to infection. Tachycardia, a common response to systemic inflammation or sepsis, was more pronounced in complicated appendicitis cases. These findings align with Sasaki et al., (2020), who reported higher body temperature and heart rate in patients with complicated appendicitis (median temperature 37.5 °C vs 37.0 °C, p&lt;0.001; median heart rate 85 vs 76 bpm, p&lt;0.001), and a higher proportion meeting tachycardia criteria (≥100 bpm: 18.4% vs 5.6%, p=0.006) (18) .
Li et al., (2019) and Kulvatunyou et al., (2019) found that delayed presentation of appendicitis increases the risk of perforation and severe complications, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis (2, 19) . In our study, the duration of symptoms was significantly longer in patients with complicated appendicitis (61.6 ± 30.7 hours) compared to those with simple appendicitis (17.6 ± 10.2 hours, p&lt;0.001). This supports the notion that longer symptom duration is associated with more severe cases.
In our study, WBC counts were significantly higher in patients with complicated appendicitis (11.5 ± 2.9 ×10³/µL) compared to simple appendicitis (7.6 ± 2.5 ×10³/µL, p &lt; 0.001), and WBC independently predicted complicated disease (OR 1.53, p &lt; 0.001). WBC levels also correlated with bilirubin, indicating a link to inflammation and cholestasis in severe cases. However, Emmanuel et al., (2011) found that hyperbilirubinemia had higher specificity than WBC or CRP for diagnosing appendicitis and its complications ( 10 ) . Giordano et al., (2013) confirmed bilirubin's usefulness for detecting perforation but noted it should be used with other clinical data and laboratory data such as WBC (20) , while Birben et al.,. (2021) found elevated bilirubin helpful in diagnosing appendicitis even with normal WBC counts (21 ).
Using &gt;0.39 mg/dL, DB separated groups (71.7% complicated vs 49.8% simple) but performed less well than TB, consistent with studies that treat DB as adjunctive rather than standalone.
Direct bilirubin showed significant positive correlations with clinical severity markers, including (duration r=0.32; temperature r=0.40; pulse r=0.45; WBC r=0.21). Notably, bilirubin did not correlate with systolic blood pressure (SBP) and showed only a minimal relationship with diastolic blood pressure (DBP, total bilirubin r=0.11). These findings align with the idea that bilirubin reflects systemic inflammation and cholestatic trends associated with severe illness.
On adjusted logistic regression, direct bilirubin remained independent predictor of complicated appendicitis with very large effect sizes (direct OR 432.2, 95% CI 92-1945; both p&lt;0.001), over and above age, comorbidities, symptom duration, temperature, pulse, and WBC (which were themselves significant). Blood pressure and sex were not retained. While the magnitude of the ORs reflects the strong separation created by the chosen cut-offs, the key point is that bilirubin retains predictive value even after accounting for other clinical and laboratory signals.
Practically, direct bilirubin &gt;0.39 mg/dL should escalate concern for complication when combined with longer symptom duration, fever, tachycardia, and leukocytosis-all of which were significantly higher in complicated cases in this cohort. Given the high NPV at ≤0.39 mg/dL, low direct bilirubin can help deprioritize urgent operative or advanced imaging in otherwise low-risk presentations, while acknowledging that bilirubin is an adjunct, not a standalone decision tool. Direct bilirubin adds incremental information but-given its lower specificity-should be interpreted alongside total bilirubin and the broader clinical picture.
## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that serum direct hyperbilirubinemia, particularly levels ≥ 0.39 mg/dL is a strong predictor of complicated appendicitis, correlating with clinical severity markers such as symptom duration, temperature, pulse, and WBC. Bilirubin testing, as a specific "rule-in" marker, complements clinical judgment and offers a cost-effective, easily accessible tool for early diagnosis and timely intervention. Our findings support the incorporation of bilirubin into routine diagnostic pathways to improve decisionmaking, optimize resource use, and reduce treatment delays.
