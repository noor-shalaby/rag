## Outcome of Conservative Treatment of Acute Appendicitis in Young Females

Emad M. Abdelrahman, Husein G. Elgohary, Abdelaziz A. Abdelaziz, Mohamed I. Abuelnasr

## Abstract:

Background: Acute appendicitis is the commonest emergency in general surgery, the treatment of it is whether  surgical  or conservative. The aim of this study is to demonstrate the effects of conservative treatment

Aim: evaluate the outcomes of conservative treatment of appendicitis in young patients. Patients and methods: this study included 92 young female patients diagnosed with acute appendicitis,  eligible  patients  will  be  recruited  from  outpatient clinic, emergency department and general surgery department of Benha  University  and  general  surgery  department  of  Benha teaching hospital. Results: With a median follow-up of nine to twelve  months,  61  (66.3%)  underwent  effective  management without  surgery  and  successfully  finished  the  original  NonOperative Treatment (NOT). In the patients who failed the NonOperative Treatment (NOT) in our trial, there were no appendiceal perforations. The findings of a recent meta-analysis, which  incorporated  several  studies  regarding  the  safety  and effectiveness  of  Non-Operative  Treatment  (NOT)  in  cases  of Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA), are in line with this. It demonstrated that Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) for Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) is successful, with a success rate of 66% at the last follow-up. Conclusion: Approximately half of appendicitis patients had successful conservative  treatment.  Patients  with  lower  age  and  body  mass index  but  higher  chest  problems  and  previous  GIT  symptoms exhibited more positive response to conservative treatment.

Keywords: Non-Operative Treatment, uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis, gastrointestinal tract.

## Introduction

Since  it  has  been  known  for  decades  that antibiotics may be used to treat appendicitis, considering the use of antibiotics  alone  instead  of  surgery  for simple cases of appendicitis has increased. While there is a good amount of evidence to support this treatment in adults, there is still more and more data showing how safe and successful non-operative management is  for  kids.  There  is  still  a  need  for  large research, which are now being carried out with findings anticipated in the next years. The  most  frequent  surgical  emergency  in children is acute appendicitis, which has a lifetime risk of 8.6% in males and 6.7% in females.  Although  appendicitis  can  occur in any stage of life, the largest prevalence occurs in those between the ages of 10 and 19 [1] .

Although the precise cause of acute appendicitis is still unknown, a number of ideas link it to lymphoid hyperplasia, mucosal inflammation, or the existence of a  fecalith.  The  latter  results  in  ischemia, infarction, supportive transmural inflammation, and appendix perforation by causing  luminal  blockage,  distention,  and inflammation of the appendix wall [2] . Fever,  colicky  periumbilical  discomfort, pain  migration  to  the  right  iliac  region, anorexia, nausea, and vomiting are among the common symptoms. However, because to their unusual appearance and poor communication abilities, preschoolers may be hard to diagnose with appendicitis. Almost all  of  the  120  US  children  (mean age  3.6  years)  who  were  diagnosed  with perforated  appendicitis  at  a  younger  age than  1  year  old  had  this  condition  at  the time of diagnosis, mostly due to delays in diagnosis [3] .

Within 24 hours of the beginning of symptoms, appendiceal perforation is anticipated to occur in 7.7% of individuals. The surgical orthodoxy that governed emergency appendectomy has undergone a paradigm change in favor of non-operative treatment.

An  84%  success  rate  in  treating adult patients with uncomplicated acute appendicitis  with  antibiotics  during  their first  hospitalization  was  found  in  a  metaanalysis  of  randomized  controlled  trials that included 5 studies and 1430 individuals. Another 21 per cent of patients were  readmitted for treatmentrelated  recurrent  appendicitis  during  the follow-up year. Comparing patients receiving antibiotic therapy to those having  an  appendectomy,  there  was  an overall 39% decrease in the risk of complications [4] .

In an observational research conducted by Abeş  and  colleagues  in  2007,  abdominal soreness  was  successfully  resolved  in  15 out  of  136  children,  aged  5  to  13,  who were chosen for non-operative therapy (93.7%)  [5] .

In  a  Swedish  randomized  controlled  trial, 50  children  (mean  age  11.2  years)  were randomly assigned to receive either intravenous (meropenem and metronidazole) or preoperative (metronidazole) antibiotic prophylaxis after  surgery  for  at  least  48  hours.  The trial's  goal  was  to  assess  the  safety  and viability of non-operative treatment of acute nonperforated appendicitis with antibiotics. The majority of patients (92%) who  received  antibiotic  treatment  saw  an early  remission  of  symptoms,  and  only  1 patient  (5%)  experienced  a  recurrence  of histologically proved acute appendicitis during the 1-year follow-up. For all included patients the protocol of treatment was the following:

For the first twelve hours after receiving intravenous (IV) liquids, patients continued to fast.  One  of  the  following  IV  antibiotics  was administered to patients: Ceftriaxone IV (1 g) once daily in combination with three daily 500 mg infusions of metronidazole IV during a 48hour period. Medication dosages are modified in  accordance with hospital policies and pain ratings.  Every  six hours, vital signs  were measured,  including  pain  levels.  The  patient was reassessed  by  a  doctor  twice  a  day.  The patient  was  sent  home  after  48  hours  if  they satisfy the predetermined discharge requirements. If not, the Non-Operative

Treatment (NOT) persisted for a maximum of 72 hours.

The  discharge  criteria  for  the  Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) group are:

Temperature &lt;38.0°C, pain ratings &lt;4, decreased WBC count, decreased CRP, ability to move, ability to tolerate oral intake, and no symptoms  of  complicated  appendicitis  were observed on the second US scan. Patients were released given three daily doses of oral metronidazole (500 mg) and three daily doses of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (1 g). The course  of  antibiotic  therapy  lasts  for  seven days in total.

## Discussion

Surgeons benefit from radiological tests to complement  the  clinical  picture  prior  to surgical  intervention,  as  the  diagnosis  of AA is still unclear.

However,  there  are  a  number  of  negative aspects to imaging, the main one being the radiation  exposure  that  young  individuals experience during a CT scan.

On the other side, individuals with an AA diagnosis  may  have  a  number  of  serious consequences from surgical procedures [7] .

Therefore, it is critical to ascertain whether surgical surgery is still the preferred course  of  treatment  for  individuals  with AA diagnoses  [8] .

Several recent research have indicated that medical  therapy  is a viable option  for managing alcoholism. The use of antibiotics as the last resort for adult-onset

arthritis (AA) is not straightforward, according to a number of recent research. These criteria include the kind of arthritis, whether the patient is an adult or a kid, and whether  the  antibiotic  is  being  used  as  a preventative  measure  or  as  a  bridge  to surgery [9].

Our  findings  showed  that  there  was  a treatment strategy for Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) patients outside surgery.

Patients who experience several  postoperative  complications,  such as surgical site infection (SSI) with Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA),  might  be  notified  that  there  is  a safe  and  generally  successful  choice  to Non-Operative  Treatment  (NOT)  [9].  In addition,  they  must  be  told  that  their  stay in the hospital will be prolonged due to the requirement for close clinical surveillance, that  in  approximately  7-8%  of  instances, Non-Operative  Treatment  (NOT)  will  fail after three days, and that in approximately be

15-16% of cases, a second round of NonOperative Treatment (NOT) may required. [10]

Typically,  over  the  follow-up  period,  1820%  of  patients  would  require  surgical care  to  remove  the  appendix  without  any further morbidities or appendiceal rupture. Based on our study results, the majority of patients  opted  Non-Operative  Treatment (NOT) to have the first hospital admission rather than the urgent procedure for Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA), even if this may  result in a lengthier hospital stay [11].

Using  predetermined  criteria,  the  current study evaluated the Non-Operative Treatment (NOT)'s safety and viability for simple  AA.  Every  single  one  of  the  92 patients  in  our  sample  who  had  an  AA diagnosis underwent Non-Operative Treatment (NOT).

Sixty-one (66.3%) of them had management  without  surgery, and they successfully finished the first NonOperative Treatment (NOT), with a median  follow-up  of  nineteen  to  twentyfour months. In the patients who failed the Non-Operative  Treatment  (NOT)  in  our trial, there were no appendiceal perforations.  This  is  in  line  with  a  recent meta-analysis's findings, which incorporated  several  studies  regarding  the safety and effectiveness of Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) in cases of Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) and demonstrated that Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) for Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) was successful with  a  success  rate  of  66%  at  the  final follow-up.

Throughout  the  follow-up  period,  there was a 14% recurrence of AA in this metaanalysis,  although  there  were  no  serious adverse events. This is consistent with our study's findings, which showed that during a  median  follow-up  period  of  19  (9-24) months, 9/67 (13.4%) of the patients in the Non-Operative Treatment  (NOT)  group experienced another AA incident. Of  the  patients  in  this  group  who  had recurrent  AA,  3\67  (4.4%)  completed  a successful second Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) trial. Conversely, in our study's surgical group, 18 out of 25 operated patients had an 80% efficaciously pathologically inflamed appendix.

Our approach of omitting patients with AA with appendicolith from the NonOperative  Treatment  (NOT)  group  and providing  IV  antibiotics  for  three  days may have had a significant role in our high success rates.

On the other hand, an inadequate course of antibiotics may result in therapeutic failure. According to the previously reported meta-analysis, there were no significant differences in outcomes between Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) and  appendectomy,  and  the  outcomes  of the two treatment methods were comparable.

The authors did note that unless there are extensive randomized trials contrasting the Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) with appendectomy in the Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA), recommendations cannot be made.

In  their  recent  systematic  review,  Kessler et al. evaluated the Non-Operative Treatment  (NOT)  and  appendectomy  for management of Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis  (UAA)  in  children  in  five cohort and randomized studies [12] . They found  that,  in  general,  there  were  a  few post-appendectomy complications, with comparable numbers in Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) and appendectomy (2% in both procedures). This puts into question  the  previously  mentioned  results and opinions [13].

But after comparing Non-Operative Treatment (NOT)'s effectiveness to appendectomy, they  discovered  that  NonOperative Treatment (NOT) was less

effective, with a success rate  of  74%

compared to appendectomy's 82.5%. Furthermore,  compared  to  Non-Operative Treatment  (NOT),  the  appendectomy  was linked to decreased readmission rates. They  came  to  the  conclusion  that,  in  the case of children with Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA), surgical intervention  should  still  be  the  primary line of therapy. Although the NonOperative  Treatment  (NOT)  group  in  our study had a greater success rate, we do not advocate  for  abandoning  surgery  for  AA since  appendectomy  is  a  permanent  cure for appendicitis.

We contend that Non-Operative Treatment (NOT) can be both safe and effective, and patients  really  prefer  it  when  we  apply stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria to specific  individuals  with  Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA). We believe  that  the  success  of  our  study was largely due to our protocol of routine follow-up,  which  in  some  cases  lasted  up to  24  months  with  a  mean  duration  of  19 months  in  the  Non-Operative  Treatment (NOT)  group  through  scheduled  visits  to the  outpatient  clinic  and  an  available  line for  patients  to  communicate  in  case  of recurrence  of  abdominal  symptoms.  The safety of the non-operative medical care of Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) is  not  disclosed by the pathology findings of the excised appendices of patients who did not pass.

Out of the 17 appendices removed because to recurrent AA, 1 had gangrenous tissue, 12 had inflammation, and 4 had no inflammation. In our research group, there was  not  a  single  instance  of  a  ruptured appendix with 100% safety. This finding is consistent with other previous studies that have demonstrated postponing surgery for Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (UAA) patients  is  safe,  does  not  raise  the  risk  of appendiceal perforation, and does not increase the risk of postoperative morbidities.

## Conclusion

Approximately half of appendicitis patients had successful conservative treatment.  Patients  with  lower  age  and body  mass  index  exhibited  more  positive response to conservative treatment.

Low  leucocyte  count,  symptoms  duration of &gt; 48 hours, anorexia, nausea and vomiting,  and  migration  of  pain  led  to successful conservative treatment in appendicitis patients.
